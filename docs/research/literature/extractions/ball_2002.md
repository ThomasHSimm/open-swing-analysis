# Ball, Best, Dowlan & Brown (2002) — Different Weight Transfer Patterns in Golf

> **Triage note:** Not on the priority-5 list, but the closest construct match in the library so far and arguably a should-keep. This IS the measurement paradigm of the planned study: dual force plates (per-foot), CoP in two axes, normalised to foot position, sampled across **8 swing events** (timing, not just magnitude), with cluster analysis used to handle inter-golfer heterogeneity. Two caveats: (1) conference paper (ISBS proceedings), lower evidence tier; (2) uploaded PDF is OCR-corrupted — Fig 1 and Table 2 absolute values are unreliable (see flags).

## Citation
Ball KA, Best RJ, Dowlan S, Brown D. Different Weight Transfer Patterns in Golf. *Proceedings of the 20th International Symposium on Biomechanics in Sports (ISBS).* Cáceres, Spain; 2002. p. 192–195. Biomechanics Unit, Victoria University, Melbourne, Australia.

## DOI
None (ISBS 2002 conference proceedings; not a DOI-registered journal article).

## Study type
Cross-sectional observational + **unsupervised classification** (cluster analysis). No intervention. Methodologically a pattern-discovery study, not a hypothesis-test of a treatment.

## Population
40 male golfers, age 19–55, **wide skill range** — professional to high handicap (handicap +2 to 26), recreational players included (min ~5 games/yr). Resulting clusters: Front Foot n=28, Reverse n=12.

## Intervention
**N/A** — observational. Manipulation is analytic (cluster classification), not applied to the golfer.

## Comparator
The two emergent clusters (Front Foot vs Reverse) compared against each other. No control condition.

## Duration
**Single session.** 10 driver swings per golfer. No training period — provides no duration guidance.

## Outcome measures
- **Equipment:** two separate AMTI force plates (one per foot), covered with synthetic grass; CoP sampled at 500 Hz (AMLAB 16-bit ADC), smoothed with 15 Hz Butterworth. Overhead camera (Peak Motus) for foot position; 200 Hz high-speed camera to identify swing events; ProV Swing Analyzer (Golftek) for clubhead and ball speed.
- **CoP normalised to foot position, two axes:**
  - **CPy% = parallel to line of shot = between-foot transfer.** 0% = back (trail) foot, 100% = front (lead) foot.
  - **CPx% = perpendicular to line of shot = heel-toe shift.** 0% = heel, 100% = toe.
- **Eight swing events** (Table 1): Takeaway (TA), Mid Backswing (MB), Late Backswing (LB), Top Backswing (TB), Early Downswing (ED), Mid Downswing (MD), Ball Contact (BC), Mid Follow-through (FT). CoP captured at each → this is the **timing dimension** the Yang study omitted.
- Derived metrics: time of maximum CPy% (as % of TA→BC), and CPx:CPy range ratio.
- Mean of 10 trials per golfer used.

## Key findings
**Two reliable clusters.** Hierarchical agglomeration showed the largest jump at the 2-cluster solution; non-hierarchical re-seeding confirmed it (largest C-Index). Validated by Point Biserial Correlation (r_pbi = 0.48, p < 0.001) and replication (both patterns recurred across three random N=28 subsets).

**Where the styles diverge — after Early Downswing:**

| Phase | Front Foot (n=28) | Reverse (n=12) |
|---|---|---|
| Backswing → ED | Similar: both start ~60% toward front foot, move to back foot, rapid forward TB→ED (CPyED ≈ 63%) | Same |
| CPy at Ball Contact | ~76% (continues toward **front/lead** foot) | ~43% (moves **back toward trail** foot) |
| CPx TB→ED | Similar: both move toward toes (CPxTB ≈ 55% → CPxED ≈ 72%) | Same |
| CPx at Ball Contact | ~65% (moves **back toward heel** — mid-foot) | ~76% (continues toward **toe**) |

So at impact: Front Foot = weight forward + mid-foot; Reverse = weight back + toward toes.

**ANOVA group differences** (significant): CPyMD (F=11.9, p<0.001, η²=0.29), CPyBC (F=50.8, p<0.001, η²=0.64), CPyFT (F=52.2, p<0.001, η²=0.66), CPxBC (F=4.5, p=0.04, η²=0.14), CPxFT (F=10.4, p=0.003, η²=0.26). Derived: time of max CPy differed (Front Foot peaks **later**, ~97% vs ~87% of TA-BC; F=29.6, p<0.001, η²≈0.40); CPx:CPy ratio differed (Front Foot 0.6 vs Reverse 0.8; F=7.66, p=0.01, η²≈0.18 — Front Foot makes more between-foot movement relative to heel-toe).

**No group difference in skill or performance:** handicap (p=0.75), clubhead velocity (p=0.57), ball velocity (p=0.66). **4 of 6 professionals were in the Reverse group.**

**Type I error demonstration:** max CPy velocity ↔ clubhead speed correlated in Front Foot (r=0.45, p=0.02) but NOT Reverse (r=0.25, p=0.48). Pooled (N=40) gave r=0.34, p=0.02 — a significant pooled result that is a **type I error for the Reverse subgroup.**

## Statistical approach
Cluster analysis: Pearson correlation similarity, between-groups linkage; two-step hierarchical→non-hierarchical (Milligan & Sokol) to eliminate nesting; optimal solution by C-Index (Milligan & Cooper). Validation: Point Biserial Correlation (Milligan), univariate ANOVA (Hair et al.), replication on random subsets (Hodge & Petlichkoff). Post-hoc correlations within and across clusters.

## Relevance to trial design
- **Appropriate training duration:** None — single session.
- **Appropriate outcome measures:** **Strong methodological precedent for your study.** Confirms dual-plate, two-axis, foot-normalised CoP across multiple swing events is a validated approach, and that **timing metrics** (time of max CPy; per-event CoP) carry large effects (η² up to 0.66) — supporting a timing-based primary outcome over magnitude alone. The 8-event scheme is a directly adoptable template.
- **Expected effect sizes:** Where real, the between-style effects are large (η² 0.29–0.66 on CPy events). But these are *between-style* differences, not *within-golfer change from training*, so they are NOT a usable effect-size anchor for your pre/post design. They tell you the measure can resolve large pattern differences, not that training moves it.
- **Likely confounders / design lessons:** Baseline weight-transfer **style** is the key lurking variable. The authors' explicit recommendation — include style groups in the design — is directly actionable for your pilot. Pooling styles risks both type I (spurious effect, as demonstrated) and type II (masked effect) errors.

## Limitations noted by authors
Kinematic analyses needed to substantiate the style interpretations (kinetics alone don't explain mechanism). Reverse pattern conflicts with coaching orthodoxy and needs further work. Important performance parameters likely differ between groups and remain to be identified.
**Reviewer-added (mine):** conference paper, not peer-reviewed journal; unbalanced clusters (28 vs 12); driver only; no power analysis; male only; PDF OCR corruption (see flag).

## Quality assessment
**Medium overall; high for conceptual/methodological relevance, lower for evidence tier.** Methodologically careful for its format — proper cluster validation (C-Index, Point Biserial, replication) rather than naïve k-means. Pulled down by: conference-proceedings status, small unbalanced groups, single-session driver-only design, and OCR-degraded source. For *your* purposes its value is the paradigm and the heterogeneity lesson, not its absolute numbers.

## Direct quotes worth keeping
Prefer paraphrase. One verbatim line on the design implication, worth keeping:
- Future golf research should include Front Foot and Reverse groups in the design "in order to avoid making type I and type II statistical errors."

## Contradiction flag
**This is the most important flag across the three papers so far.**
1. **Performance-payoff rationale is directly undercut.** Weight-transfer style — measured exactly as you plan to measure it — is unrelated to handicap, clubhead speed, or ball speed. Two distinct styles are both effective, and the "wrong" style by coaching orthodoxy (Reverse) was used by 4 of 6 pros. If your study's framing implies a single correct CoP pattern or a performance benefit from shifting weight transfer, this is the strongest counter-evidence in the library and it's on-construct.
2. **Intervention-direction risk.** If your training nudges golfers toward a front-foot / earlier-transfer ideal, you may be moving naturally effective Reverse-style golfers *away* from their efficient pattern. Consider whether your outcome should be "movement toward a target pattern" at all, vs. within-style consistency/timing quality.
3. **Small-pilot analysis risk (demonstrated, not hypothetical).** Pooling styles produced a real type I error here. With a small pilot you could (a) manufacture an apparent intervention effect driven by a couple of style outliers, or (b) mask a real effect averaged across opposing styles. Mitigations: classify baseline style before analysis (even crudely, given small n), report within-subject change rather than group means where possible, and pre-register how style heterogeneity will be handled.
4. **Source-reliability caveat:** Table 2 absolute performance values (clubhead ~156, ball ~224, labelled m/s) are physically implausible for a golf driver and the PDF is OCR-corrupted — do NOT cite those absolute numbers. The statistical comparisons (no skill/speed difference; significant timing/pattern differences) are what to cite, and they are robust to the unit problem.
