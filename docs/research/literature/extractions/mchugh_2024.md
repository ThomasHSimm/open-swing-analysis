# McHugh et al. (2024) — Kinematic, Kinetic & Temporal Metrics Associated With Golf Proficiency

> **Triage note:** Not on the priority-5 list, but the highest-quality and most on-construct paper in the library so far: peer-reviewed (JSCR), dual force plates, powered design, full timing analysis. Cross-sectional, so no trainability evidence. **Primary value:** peer-reviewed support that GRF/weight-transfer *timing* and *unweighting* discriminate skill where GRF *magnitude* does not — directly informs choosing a timing-based primary outcome. Secondary value: reinforces that the dominant performance driver is trunk rotation (x-factor), not weight transfer.

## Citation
McHugh MP, O'Mahoney CA, Orishimo KF, Kremenic IJ, Nicholas SJ. Kinematic, Kinetic, and Temporal Metrics Associated With Golf Proficiency. *J Strength Cond Res.* 2024;38(3):599–606. (Nicholas Institute of Sports Medicine, Lenox Hill Hospital, NY.)

## DOI
10.1519/JSC.0000000000004663

## Study type
Cross-sectional, three-group comparison (proficient / average / unskilled) + correlation/stepwise multiple regression. No intervention.

## Population
33 male golfers; age 37 ± 14 (range 18–72); height 176 ± 7 cm; mass 80.2 ± 16.6 kg; USGA index 8.1 ± 7.8. Inclusion: handicap ≤24, ≥6 rounds/yr, uninjured, ≥18 yr. Female golfers excluded (assumed biomechanical differences). Groups (n=11 each) by tertiles on a composite of handicap index, ball speed, total distance:
- **Proficient:** top tertile on ≥2 of 3 factors, not bottom on the third (n=11).
- **Unskilled:** bottom tertile on ≥2 of 3, not top on the third (n=11).
- **Average:** remainder (n=11).

## Intervention
**N/A** — observational.

## Comparator
The three proficiency groups vs each other.

## Duration
**Single session.** 5 driver shots, best 3 by distance analysed. No training period — no duration guidance.

## Outcome measures
- **Kinematics:** 10-camera motion capture @ 500 Hz, 24 body markers + 5 club + ball. 14 metrics: 9 positional (3 x-factor [top of backswing, peak, impact] + 6 hip ROM), 3 peak velocities (pelvis, trunk, lead arm), 2 average accelerations (pelvis, trunk).
- **Kinetics:** dual force plates @ 1000 Hz. Resultant GRF (lead & back foot) at peak, top of backswing, impact; expressed as % body weight. Plus lead-foot **unweighting** (peak→impact GRF drop).
- **Temporal:** timing (ms) of each kinetic/kinematic peak relative to top of backswing (=0); plus event **sequence order**.
- **Proficiency (DV):** USGA handicap index, ball speed (TrackMan), total distance.
- **Power analysis done a priori** (80% power, α=0.05, ~11/group to detect ~20% x-factor / 18% trunk velocity / 30% GRF differences).

## Key findings
**Identifies PROFICIENT (sig. better than both average + unskilled):**
- X-factor at impact: 36±5° vs 27±6° (avg) vs 21±6° (unskilled), p<0.001, ES up to 2.6.
- Trunk deceleration (peak velocity→impact): proficient 83% > average, 220% > unskilled, p=0.001.

**Identifies UNSKILLED (sig. worse than both):**
- X-factor at top of backswing (41±6° vs 54–56°) and peak x-factor (46±6° vs 57–62°), p<0.001.
- Timing of peak lead-foot GRF (~43% later) and peak lead-arm velocity (~32–35% later), p<0.05.

**Magnitude of GRF did NOT discriminate skill** — back foot (peak p=0.130, impact p=0.872) and lead foot (peak p=0.096, impact p=0.082) all n.s. BUT lead-foot **unweighting** did (76.2% / 64.7% / 28.8% body mass; p=0.008, extremes only). Pattern: proficient golfers loaded lead foot fast (peak at 192 ms vs 271 ms unskilled) then unloaded sharply before impact.

**Sequence ORDER of events did NOT differ by skill** (all sequencing χ² p>0.05) — only absolute *timing* did. Proximal-to-distal kinematic sequence held across all groups.

**Velocity:** peak pelvis velocity discriminated extremes (p=0.012); peak trunk (p=0.199) and arm (p=0.144) velocity did NOT.

**Regression (DV variance explained):**
| DV | Predictors (stepwise) | R² |
|---|---|---|
| Ball speed | x-factor@impact, peak pelvis vel, time-to-peak-arm-vel, time-to-peak-trunk-vel, peak lead-foot GRF | 84.5% |
| Total distance | x-factor@impact, peak lead-arm vel, peak lead-foot GRF | 74.3% |
| Handicap index | x-factor@impact, peak lead-arm vel | 62.7% (68.2% with age) |
X-factor at impact was the single strongest predictor of all three.

## Statistical approach
One-way ANOVA + Tukey post-hoc per metric; classification scheme based on which pairwise contrasts were significant. χ² for sequence proportions. Pearson r for associations. Stepwise multiple regression (entry at bivariate p<0.10). Effect sizes with 95% CI reported.

## Relevance to trial design
- **Training duration:** None.
- **Outcome measures — strong, citable steer:** the metrics that discriminated skill were **temporal** (timing of peak lead-foot GRF) and **rate-of-change** (lead-foot unweighting), NOT static GRF magnitude. This is peer-reviewed support for making your primary outcome CoP/GRF *timing* and *unweighting rate*, not displacement/loading magnitude. The composite proficiency definition (handicap + ball speed + distance) is also a defensible model for stratifying your participants.
- **Expected effect sizes:** large between-group ES (1.0–2.6), but these are *cross-sectional group differences*, not *within-subject training change*. Do NOT use as a training-effect anchor — they will overstate what a pre/post intervention can move.
- **Likely confounders:** age (proficient 28±6 vs unskilled 48±15 yr, p<0.001 — partly confounds the group contrast, though age dropped out of the ball-speed/distance regressions). GRF magnitude not tracking skill is itself a design lesson: don't power your study to detect magnitude shifts.

## Limitations noted by authors
Proficient golfers younger than unskilled (age confound, acknowledged). Female golfers excluded (generalisability). Driver only — unknown whether relationships hold for irons (directly relevant: your weight-transfer work may use irons, à la Yang). Cross-sectional — proficiency associations, not causal/trainable.
**Reviewer-added (mine):** n=11/group is small for ~30 metrics with no family-wise correction (false-positive risk on the p=0.03–0.05 findings); ball-speed regression R²=0.845 is from 5 stepwise predictors on n=33 with raw R² only (no adjusted R²/CV) → optimistically inflated, in-sample fit not predictive performance.

## Quality assessment
**High** for the core findings (x-factor at impact, trunk deceleration, lead-foot timing/unweighting: large ES, a priori power, dual-plate, peer-reviewed). **Medium** for the secondary metrics and regression: multiple-comparison and overfitting concerns mean the marginal findings and the headline R² should be read conservatively. Best-quality on-construct source in the set.

## Direct quotes worth keeping
Prefer paraphrase. One line on the training implication, worth verbatim:
- The authors conclude x-factor at impact and trunk deceleration "points to the potential of axial trunk rotation training to improve golf performance."

## Contradiction flag
**Mixed: supports your timing outcome, undercuts a magnitude/weight-transfer-centric rationale.**
1. **Supports (the useful part):** weight-transfer *timing* and lead-foot *unweighting* carry a real, large skill signal in a high-quality dual-plate study. This is the first peer-reviewed, on-construct backing for a timing-based CoP outcome — use it to justify that design choice over magnitude.
2. **Undercuts:** GRF *magnitude* (peak, at impact, either foot) did NOT discriminate skill. If your study's outcome or rationale centres on how much weight shifts or peak loading, this paper is direct counter-evidence. Re-centre on timing/unweighting.
3. **Reinforces the cross-paper pattern:** the dominant performance driver here (and in the regressions) is **x-factor / trunk rotation**, not weight transfer — and the authors' own training recommendation is trunk rotation, not balance. Consistent with Brennan (balance trivial), Yang (club manipulation didn't move COG), Ball (weight-transfer style unrelated to skill). Across four papers, the weight-transfer→performance link is weak-to-absent on magnitude; the defensible signal is timing.
4. **Design implication:** frame weight-transfer/CoP timing as a movement-quality outcome in its own right; if you want a performance hook, x-factor-at-impact is the better-evidenced target, and you could consider measuring it alongside CoP to position your work against this literature rather than ignoring it.
