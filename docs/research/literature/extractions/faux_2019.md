# Faux et al. 2019 — Effect of Alterations in Foot CoP on Lower Body Kinematics: Five-Iron Golf Swing

**Citation**: Faux, L.; Carlisle, A.; Vickers, J.; Diss, C. The effect of alterations in foot centre of pressure on lower body kinematics during the five-iron golf swing. *Journal of Sports Sciences*, 2019, 37(17), 2014–2020.

**DOI**: 10.1080/02640414.2019.1614714

**Study type**: Mixed design — immediate pre-post intervention (group, n=8) + longitudinal case study (n=1, one-year programme). Not an RCT; no control group.

**Population**: 8 male PGA-qualified golf coaches, all zero handicap. Age 45±6 years, height 1.81±0.05m, mass 89.13±6.66kg. All highly skilled, right-handed. One participant returned after one year for the longitudinal component.

**Intervention**:

*Immediate*: Single verbal instruction to increase medial foot pressure during the swing — specifically, vertical pressure onto the medial portion of the trail foot prior to backswing initiation (slight pronation), then medial pressure on the lead foot prior to downswing initiation. Visual demonstration provided. Participants performed 5–10 swings immediately after.

*Longitudinal (case study)*: 30 coaching sessions over one year (~2–3/month) delivered by the lead researcher (a golf specialist), using the same medial pressure cue with visual demonstration and verbal instruction. Same laboratory setup replicated at one-year follow-up.

**Comparator**: None. Within-subject pre-post only. No control group, no sham condition.

**Duration**: Immediate intervention — single session. Longitudinal — one year, ~30 sessions.

**Outcome measures**:
- %GRF distribution (resultant GRF as % body weight) on lead and trail sides, throughout backswing and downswing phases, time-normalised to 100%
- 3D lower body joint kinematics: ankle (sagittal, frontal, transverse planes), knee (frontal plane: varus/valgus), hip (transverse plane: internal/external rotation) at address (ADD), top of backswing (ToBS), and ball impact (BI)
- Club head velocity at BI (m/s)
- Club angle at BI (degrees)
- Equipment: 9-camera (100Hz) Vicon MXF20 motion capture + 2× Kistler 9281CA force plates (1000Hz). Upgraded to 12-camera for one-year follow-up.

**Key findings**:

*Immediate intervention (n=8)*:
- No significant changes in %GRF waveforms (SPM analysis)
- Club head velocity: 31.02→30.63 m/s (−1.26%, ES=0.23) — essentially unchanged
- Club angle at BI: 7.47°→6.62° (−7.65%, ES=0.22) — unchanged
- Some significant ankle kinematic changes at ToBS (trail ankle dorsi-/plantar flexion, inversion/eversion, rotation)
- Lead ankle inversion/eversion and rotation at BI showed significant changes
- Lead and trail knee valgus showed some significant changes
- Trail hip rotation changes at ToBS significant
- Overall: immediate verbal cue produced limited but some kinematic changes; no GRF change; no performance change

*Longitudinal case study (n=1)*:
- **Significant change in lead side %GRF in final 9% of downswing** (p=0.015, t*=6.047) — change occurring 0.26s before impact
- Weight distribution at BI shifted from ~60% lead/40% trail to approximately **50/50** — authors frame this as CoP becoming central to base of support, in line with CoM
- Club head velocity: 31.22→31.39 m/s (+0.54%, ES=0.32) — marginal increase
- Club angle at BI: 7.95°→1.69° (−14.09%, ES=0.53) — flatter club face
- Large kinematic changes across ankle, knee and hip in all planes (Hedges' g values very large: 3–25, though n=1 so these are effect sizes within a single subject across 5 trials, not between groups)
- Trail ankle reached tri-planar neutral at ToBS — interpreted as improved foot-ground interface stability at backswing-to-downswing transition
- Trail ankle external rotation at BI increased severely (36°) — flagged as potentially injury-inducing
- Lead knee varus reduced by ~12° — improved alignment
- Trail hip internal rotation reduced ~60% during backswing — interpreted as improved stability

**Statistical approach**: Shapiro-Wilk for normality. Cohen's d effect sizes corrected to Hedges' g for small sample. **Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM)** with paired t-test for all waveform data — continuous analysis across 100% time-normalised swing phases. Discrete joint angles reported as mean(SD) and % difference. No correction for multiple comparisons.

## Relevance to Trial Design

**Closest methodological precedent**: This is the only peer-reviewed study that (a) applies a foot CoP intervention to golfers, (b) measures GRF distribution and lower body kinematics simultaneously, and (c) includes both an immediate and a longitudinal component. It is the direct precedent for your trial.

**Training duration**: The immediate verbal intervention produced minimal GRF change. The one-year programme produced significant GRF change. This is a critical finding — it suggests that a short intervention (days or weeks) may not be sufficient to change GRF patterns in experienced golfers. Your 4-week trial duration sits between these two extremes; whether it is sufficient is genuinely uncertain based on this evidence.

**Outcome measures**: %GRF distribution across the downswing phase (time-normalised, SPM analysis) is exactly the type of CoP timing outcome your trial intends to measure. The significant change occurred in the **final 9% of the downswing** (~0.26s before impact) — this is the critical window, not a global phase change. This informs where to focus your primary outcome window.

**Expected effect sizes**: No pooled effect sizes available. The immediate intervention showed ES=0.22–0.23 on club head velocity (trivial). The longitudinal intervention showed ES=0.32–0.53 on club velocity (small-moderate) and very large effects on joint kinematics in the case study (g=3–25, but these are within-subject across 5 trials, not between subjects, so not comparable to standard effect sizes). No effect size reported for the %GRF change itself.

**Population**: Zero handicap PGA coaches — substantially more skilled than the population relevant to your trial. If highly skilled golfers with deeply ingrained mechanics require one year to show GRF change, recreational or intermediate golfers (handicap 10–20) may respond faster (less entrenched patterns) or may be harder to detect (higher baseline variability). The direction is uncertain.

**SPM is the appropriate analysis method**: The authors use SPM explicitly for continuous GRF waveform analysis, consistent with Watson et al.'s recommendation. This validates SPM as the standard for this type of data.

**50/50 weight distribution at impact**: The authors reframe what "good" GRF looks like — challenging the traditional recommendation of 75–80% lead leg at impact, arguing that 50/50 with CoP central to base of support represents superior stability. This has implications for how you define your outcome target.

## Limitations

**Noted by authors**: Small sample (n=8 immediate); camera sampling frequency (100Hz) lower than ideal; ball tracking not possible within camera field of view; case study approach limits generalisability.

**Additional limitations not acknowledged**:
- No control group — cannot distinguish intervention effect from practice effect (participants performed 5–10 additional swings)
- Immediate intervention confounds verbal/cognitive instruction with proprioceptive change — unclear what mechanism produced kinematic changes
- Case study (n=1) provides compelling data but no statistical inference is valid beyond within-subject comparisons; Hedges' g values of 3–25 in a single subject across 5 trials are not interpretable as population effect sizes
- 30 coaching sessions over one year is a complex multi-component intervention — impossible to isolate what produced the GRF change
- Zero handicap PGA coaches are a ceiling population — both for proprioceptive sensitivity and for potential performance improvement
- No reliability data reported for any outcome measure
- Club head velocity measured but no launch monitor data — ball speed, carry distance, and accuracy not captured
- SPM analysis without multiple comparison correction across many joint angles and time points

---

## Quality Assessment

**Medium**. Published in a respected journal (J Sports Sci), uses appropriate continuous analysis (SPM), and is the only paper to directly manipulate foot CoP and measure GRF in golf. However, n=8 with no control group, and the most compelling finding (GRF change) comes from a single case study. Results are suggestive rather than confirmatory. The one-year longitudinal component is genuinely novel and the finding about the final 9% of downswing is specific and plausible.

## Flags for Trial Design

1. **Immediate verbal cue alone is insufficient to change GRF in skilled golfers.** The group (n=8, immediate) showed no significant GRF change. Only the one-year programme produced GRF change. For your trial: if your population is less skilled (more room for improvement), you may see earlier change; but if your intervention is shorter than 4–6 weeks, the Faux data would predict null GRF results. Plan for this in your power calculation and discussion.

2. **The critical time window is the final 9% of downswing (~0.26s before impact).** Don't average GRF/CoP across the whole swing — this will dilute the signal. Define your primary outcome window as the transition and final downswing phase.

3. **50/50 at impact may be a better target than 75/80% lead.** This reframes the traditional coaching recommendation. If you're measuring %GRF at impact, your reference value for "improved" should probably be movement toward 50/50, not toward the traditional 75–80% standard.

4. **No control group is the paper's central weakness — don't replicate it.** The practice effect confound (extra swings in the post condition) is uncontrolled. Your trial needs either a waitlist control or an active comparator with matched swing volume.

5. **SPM is your analysis method of choice for GRF waveforms.** Faux et al. established it as appropriate for this data type; Watson et al. 2026 explicitly recommended it. Use it, and reference both papers in your methods justification.

6. **The Richards et al. 1985 paper** (cited here — "more efficient and rapid weight shift on the lead foot after adjustments to the centre of pressure to various points on the foot") is the historical precursor to this line of work. Worth locating as background context.
