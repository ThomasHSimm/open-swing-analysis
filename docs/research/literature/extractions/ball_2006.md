# Ball 2006 — Weight Transfer Styles in the Golf Swing

**Citation**: Ball, K. (2006). *Weight Transfer Styles in the Golf Swing: Individual and Group Analysis* [PhD thesis, Victoria University].

**DOI**: None found in the thesis PDF. The thesis states that Study 1 and Study 2 supported later publications by Ball and Best in *Journal of Sports Sciences*, but the uploaded document itself is the 2006 PhD thesis.

**Study type**: Observational / cross-sectional biomechanics thesis with three linked studies:
- Study 1: cross-sectional cluster analysis of centre of pressure patterns.
- Study 2: group-based association between centre of pressure parameters and club velocity.
- Study 3: individual-based repeated-shot analysis with curve fitting and exploratory non-linear analysis.

**Population**:

Study 1 and Study 2:
- n = 62 golfers.
- Skill range: professional players, low-handicap, high-handicap, and recreational golfers.
- Mean handicap 11.1 ± 8.0, range +2 to 28, plus 6 recreational golfers without handicaps.
- Age 34.1 ± 13.7 years, range 15–63.
- Height 1.81 ± 0.07 m.
- Mass 81.7 ± 9.1 kg.
- Both right- and left-handed golfers were included.
- Sex not clearly stated in the extracted thesis text.

Study 3:
- n = 5 golfers.
- Skill range: professional, handicap 3, handicap 14, handicap 5, and one social golfer.
- Age range 24–36 years.
- Three classified as Reverse style and two as Front Foot style.
- Each golfer performed 50 simulated drives.

**Intervention**: None. This was not a training intervention. Golfers performed simulated driver swings while standing on force plates.

**Comparator**:
- No control group.
- Main analytic comparator was between naturally occurring weight-transfer styles:
  - Front Foot style.
  - Reverse style.
- Study 3 also compared relationships within individual golfers across repeated shots.

**Duration**:
- Single testing session for each study.
- Study 1 / Study 2: 10 simulated driver swings per golfer.
- Study 3: 50 simulated driver swings per golfer.
- No training period and no follow-up.

**Outcome measures**:

- **Centre of pressure parallel to the line of shot (CPy / CPy%)**:
  - Measured from two force plates, one under each foot.
  - Expressed relative to foot position: 0% = back/trail foot, 100% = front/lead foot.
  - Used as the study's operational measure of golf “weight transfer”.

- **CPy% at eight swing events**:
  - Takeaway (TA)
  - Mid backswing (MB)
  - Late backswing (LB)
  - Top backswing (TB)
  - Early downswing (ED)
  - Mid downswing (MD)
  - Ball contact (BC)
  - Mid follow-through (MF)

- **CPy velocity**:
  - Instantaneous velocity of centre of pressure at specified swing events, particularly in downswing and around ball contact.

- **Derived CPy parameters**:
  - Minimum CPy%.
  - Maximum CPy%.
  - CPy range.
  - Timing of maximum CPy%.
  - Other timing/range parameters used in group and individual analyses.

- **Club Velocity**:
  - Club velocity at ball contact.
  - Used as the primary performance measure instead of handicap.

- **Descriptive covariates**:
  - Handicap, age, height, mass.
  - Study 3 also included playing experience.

**Key findings**:

- Two main centre-of-pressure / weight-transfer styles were identified:
  - **Front Foot style**: CP moved toward the back foot during the backswing, moved forward during early downswing, and continued toward the front/lead foot through ball contact.
  - **Reverse style**: CP moved toward the back foot during the backswing and forward during early downswing, but then moved back toward the trail/back foot by ball contact.

- Both styles occurred across a wide skill range, including skilled golfers. The Reverse style should not automatically be treated as a technical fault.

- There were no meaningful differences between the main style groups in handicap or club velocity at ball contact, so style alone did not separate better and worse golfers.

- For the **Front Foot group**, larger CP range and more rapid CP movement during downswing were associated with higher club velocity.

- For the **Reverse group**, positioning CP further from the back foot at late backswing and transferring CP more rapidly back toward the trail/back foot at ball contact were associated with higher club velocity.

- Study 3 found that all five individual golfers showed significant relationships between CP parameters and club velocity, but the specific useful parameter differed by golfer.

- CP range was the most consistent individual-level parameter, being related to club velocity for all five golfers.

- Non-linear analysis using Poincaré plots appeared useful for some golfers because it captured shot-to-shot and longer-term variability patterns that ordinary linear summaries could miss.

**Statistical approach**:

- Study 1:
  - Hierarchical cluster analysis.
  - Non-hierarchical cluster analysis.
  - Cluster validation using point-biserial correlation, C-index, replication subsets, leave-one-out reclassification, and theoretical assessment.
  - Group comparisons using ANOVA or equivalent post-hoc comparisons where appropriate.

- Study 2:
  - Correlation analysis between CP parameters and club velocity.
  - Partial correlations controlling for age.
  - Parameter reduction using cluster analysis.
  - Best-subsets regression.
  - Multiple regression.
  - Outlier and influence diagnostics.

- Study 3:
  - Individual-level curve estimation between CP parameters and club velocity.
  - Linear and non-linear relationships assessed per golfer.
  - Poincaré plots used as exploratory non-linear analysis for CP range variability.
  - Autocorrelation checks for residual independence.

**Relevance to trial design**:

- **Appropriate training duration**:
  - The thesis gives no direct evidence for training duration because it is not an intervention study.
  - It does, however, support collecting enough swings per golfer. Ten swings were used in the main group studies and 50 swings in the individual study.
  - For a pilot trial, this strengthens the case for collecting more than a very small number of swings per session and for checking within-subject stability.

- **Appropriate outcome measures**:
  - Strong support for measuring CP / CoP across multiple swing events, not only at address, top of backswing, and impact.
  - CP timing, CP range, and CP velocity appear more informative than a single static impact value.
  - Style classification or at least style description should be included before interpreting pre/post changes.
  - Club velocity is a useful performance measure, but accuracy and consistency were not captured and should be considered in future designs.

- **Expected effect sizes**:
  - No intervention effect sizes.
  - Cross-sectional associations and group/individual relationships should not be used directly as expected training effects.
  - The thesis is better used to justify outcome selection and heterogeneity handling than to power a training trial.

- **Likely confounders**:
  - Individual weight-transfer style.
  - Skill level and handicap.
  - Age.
  - Stance width and foot position normalisation.
  - Swing event detection and timing error.
  - Shot-to-shot variability.
  - Left- vs right-handed golfers.
  - Club velocity as an incomplete performance measure because it ignores accuracy and strike quality.
  - Grouping all golfers together when different movement strategies exist.

**Limitations noted**:

- The thesis repeatedly argues that previous literature was limited by:
  - Use of handicap rather than direct swing performance measures.
  - Too few trials per golfer.
  - Too few swing events.
  - Failure to identify different weight-transfer styles.
  - Lack of individual-based analysis.

- Limitations of the thesis itself:
  - No intervention, so it cannot show whether training can change CP patterns.
  - Indoor simulated drives into a net, not on-course performance.
  - Performance measured mainly as club velocity, not accuracy, carry, dispersion, or scoring.
  - Kinematic data were not included, limiting explanation of the underlying mechanics of the styles.
  - Study 3 had only five golfers because of testing delays.
  - Study 3 used manual event identification and did not precisely gen-lock video and force-plate timing.
  - Sex composition is not clearly stated in the extracted text.
  - The Reverse style mechanism was identified but not biomechanically explained.

**Quality assessment**: Medium-high for measurement and construct development; medium for direct trial-design relevance.

Reason: This is a detailed PhD thesis with a strong methodological contribution for golf weight-transfer measurement. It uses more trials and more swing events than much earlier work, uses direct club velocity rather than handicap alone, and explicitly handles movement-style heterogeneity. The main weakness for the proposed trial is that it is observational: it cannot establish whether balance training changes CoP timing or whether such change improves performance.

**Direct quotes worth keeping**:

> “neither style was a technical error”

> “requires styles to be identified prior to any performance analysis”

**Flag anything that directly contradicts assumptions in our experiment design**:

1. **There may not be one “correct” weight-transfer pattern.** The thesis identifies Front Foot and Reverse styles across skill levels, with no clear handicap or club-velocity superiority for one style. A trial should avoid assuming all golfers should be trained toward a single forward-transfer template.

2. **A Reverse pattern is not automatically bad.** For Reverse golfers, faster movement of CP back toward the trail foot at ball contact was associated with better club velocity. That directly conflicts with a simple “more pressure forward at impact is better” assumption.

3. **Grouping everyone together can produce misleading results.** The thesis argues that failing to account for style can create both false positive and false negative interpretations. A pre/post pilot should either stratify by baseline style or pre-specify style as an exploratory moderator.

4. **Impact-only measures are too crude.** The thesis supports measuring CP at multiple swing events and examining range, timing, and velocity. A single impact-pressure outcome would miss much of the relevant movement pattern.

5. **Club velocity is not enough.** The thesis used club velocity as a better performance measure than handicap, but it also points toward the need for accuracy and consistency. A trial claiming performance improvement should include ball speed / carry / dispersion or strike-quality outcomes if available.

6. **Individual analysis matters.** The five-golfer repeated-shot study suggests the relevant CP-performance relationship can be golfer-specific. A feasibility study should report individual trajectories rather than only a group mean.
