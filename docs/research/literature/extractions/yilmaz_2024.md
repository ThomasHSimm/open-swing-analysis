# Yılmaz et al. 2024 — Proprioceptive Training Sports Performance: Systematic Review

**Citation**: Yılmaz, O.; Soylu, Y.; Erkmen, N.; Kaplan, T.; Batalik, L. Effects of proprioceptive training on sports performance: a systematic review. *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation* 2024, 16, 149.

**DOI**: 10.1186/s13102-024-00936-z

**Study type**: Systematic review (PRISMA-compliant, no meta-analysis)

**Population**: 19 included studies, 621 participants total. Ages 12–24 years. Mixed sex. Sports: soccer (263), basketball (132), athletes/track (109), taekwondo (30), speed skaters (28), handball (22), volleyball (18), fencing (19). All athletes; no sedentary/general population studies included.

**Intervention**: Proprioceptive training interventions across included studies. Forms include: balance board/disc standing, single-leg stance (eyes open/closed), medicine ball throws on unstable surfaces, ankle disk exercises, balance cushion work. One study examined acute effects; the remainder ranged from 6–20 weeks. Training frequency 3–5 days/week. Session duration 10–60 min.

**Comparator**: Most included studies used a control group receiving regular sport-specific training without proprioceptive augmentation. Some used active comparators (e.g., muscular strength training vs proprioceptive training).

**Duration**: Across included studies — minimum 6 weeks, maximum 20 weeks, with the majority at 8–12 weeks. Most common: 8 weeks, 3 days/week.

**Outcome measures**: Highly heterogeneous across included studies. Categories:
- Static and dynamic balance (Y-Balance Test, Biodex Dynamic Stability, Balance Error Scoring System, Star Excursion Balance Test, postural sway platforms)
- Joint position sense (knee, ankle)
- Explosive strength (vertical/horizontal/lateral jump, countermovement jump, squat jump)
- Speed (20m sprint, 30m sprint, shuttle run)
- Agility (Illinois Agility, Pro-Agility, Arrowhead, square agility)
- Technical sports skills (dribbling, passing, shooting, ball control — sport-specific)
- Physiological capacity (VO2 max, body fat)
- Muscle activation and coactivation
- Chronic joint instability assessment

No studies in the review measured centre of pressure timing or dynamics during sport-specific movement execution (e.g., during a swing, throw, or kick). Balance outcomes were all static or quasi-static.

**Key findings**:

*Balance and postural stability* (9/19 studies): Consistent positive effects across sports and populations. Improvements in both static and dynamic balance reported in soccer, basketball, fencing, skating, taekwondo, volleyball.

*Technical skills* (multiple studies): Improvements in passing, dribbling, shooting, and ball control reported in soccer and basketball. Effect appears mediated through improved proprioceptive acuity and neuromuscular control rather than strength per se.

*Explosive power and muscle activation* (4 studies): Positive effects on jump performance and muscle activation. One study (Romero-Franco et al.) showed improved medial-lateral postural balance and jump performance in sprinters after 6 weeks.

*Agility and speed* (3 studies): Mixed. Agility improved in several soccer studies; speed improvements were less consistent and some studies showed no effect.

*Null findings worth noting*: Viran & Canlı (2022) found no improvement in eye-hand coordination, vertical jump, core endurance, agility, sprint, or balance after 8-week proprioceptive training in soccer players — only passing and ball control improved. This contradicts the review's broadly positive framing.

**Statistical approach**: Narrative synthesis only — no pooled effect sizes, no meta-analysis. TESTEX scale used for methodological quality assessment (12-item scale; scores: <4 poor, 4–7 moderate, 8–11 good, >11 excellent). Quality range across included studies: moderate to excellent (scores 6–11). Most studies scored 7–9. Key weaknesses across studies: no allocation concealment (criterion 3: all zeros), no assessor blinding (criterion 5: all zeros), no intention-to-treat analysis (criterion 7: mostly zeros).

**Search window**: January 2010 – October 2022. Published July 2024.

## Relevance to Trial Design

**Training duration**: The evidence base clusters around 8–12 weeks for most balance and performance outcomes. The one 4-week study (Li et al., not in this review) is an outlier — the broader literature uses longer periods. If your trial uses 4–6 weeks, you are at the short end of what is evidenced. 8 weeks would be better supported.

**Appropriate outcome measures**: The review confirms that static/dynamic balance tests (Y-Balance, Biodex, sway platforms) are standard outcome measures for proprioceptive training studies. Notably absent across all 19 studies: no CoP measurement *during* sport-specific movement. This gap directly supports the novelty claim of measuring CoP timing during the golf swing, but also means there is no reference data for expected effect sizes on that specific outcome.

**Expected effect sizes**: No pooled effect sizes reported — this is a significant limitation of the review for your purposes. Individual studies show large improvements in balance metrics (30–50% range in some cases), consistent with Li et al. 2024. Effect sizes for technical performance outcomes (passing accuracy, swing speed) are smaller and less consistent. You cannot extract a reliable expected effect size for CoP-during-swing from this review.

**Likely confounders**: Training volume differences between groups (noted inconsistently across studies); lack of blinding; sport-specific skill practice confounding proprioceptive training effects; age and maturation effects in youth samples (most studies are 12–24 year range).

**Sports coverage**: No golf studies included. All 19 studies are team/individual sports with fundamentally different movement demands (multi-directional, reactive) compared to golf (cyclical, self-initiated). Extrapolation requires caution.

---

## Limitations

**Noted by authors**: Diverse terminology complicated search; only English-language peer-reviewed publications included; no meta-analysis performed (heterogeneity of outcomes precluded pooling); generalisability limited by sport-specific focus of included studies.

**Additional limitations not acknowledged**:
- Search cut-off October 2022 means ~2 years of literature excluded by publication date
- TESTEX quality scores are relatively generous — all studies scored zero on allocation concealment and assessor blinding, which are fundamental RCT quality criteria; calling studies with these gaps "Good" overstates confidence
- Narrative synthesis with 19 heterogeneous studies and broadly positive framing risks confirmation bias — the null findings (e.g., Viran & Canlı) are mentioned but not weighted against positive findings
- No quantification of heterogeneity; pooling narrative conclusions across studies with 10-min to 60-min sessions and 6- to 20-week durations is of limited precision
- Age range 12–24 is mostly youth/young adult; limited applicability to older recreational golfers

---

## Quality Assessment

**Medium** as an evidence synthesis. PRISMA-compliant and covers the relevant literature systematically, but absence of meta-analysis and inability to extract effect sizes limits quantitative usefulness. The included primary studies are mostly moderate-to-good quality RCTs, but universal absence of blinding and allocation concealment across the field is a systemic weakness the review acknowledges only briefly.

## Flags for Trial Design

1. **No CoP-during-movement outcomes anywhere in this literature.** The review covers 19 studies and zero measure CoP timing or weight transfer dynamics during sport execution. This confirms the novelty of your intended primary outcome but also means you have no precedent for expected effect size or test-retest reliability of that measure in a training study context.

2. **8 weeks is the modal duration in this evidence base, not 4.** If your trial uses 4 weeks, you should explicitly justify the shorter duration (e.g., citing Li et al.) and acknowledge that the broader proprioceptive training literature uses longer periods. Power calculations based on 4-week effects may be optimistic.

3. **No golf studies exist in this literature.** The review is entirely team/racket/individual sports. Golf's unilateral, self-paced, cyclical movement pattern is meaningfully different from the reactive balance demands tested in soccer, basketball, and fencing studies. Direct transfer of effect size estimates is not justified.

4. **Volume confound is systemic across the field.** Most included studies do not match training volume between groups. This is a known weakness — worth explicitly controlling in your design.

5. **Technical skill improvements are real but mechanism is unclear.** The review reports improved passing, dribbling, and shooting alongside balance improvements, but cannot establish whether balance improvements *cause* technical improvements or whether both reflect a general training effect. For golf, this matters: improved CoP control may not translate to improved swing performance through a simple causal chain.
