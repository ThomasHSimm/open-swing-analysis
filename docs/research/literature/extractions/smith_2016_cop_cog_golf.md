# Smith et al. 2016 — COG/COP patterns in golf swing

**Citation**: Smith, A. C., Roberts, J. R., Kong, P. W., & Forrester, S. E. (2016). *Comparison of centre of gravity and centre of pressure patterns in the golf swing*. *European Journal of Sport Science*. DOI: 10.1080/17461391.2016.1240238.

**DOI**: 10.1080/17461391.2016.1240238

**Study type**: Cross-sectional / observational biomechanics study.

**Population**: 22 right-handed low-handicap golfers; handicap range +3 to 4; age 26 ± 7 years; height 179.5 ± 7.3 cm; mass 79.4 ± 13.1 kg. Participants were either university team golfers or professional golfers from local clubs.

**Intervention**: None. This was not a training intervention. Golfers performed driver swings in a laboratory while centre of pressure, centre of gravity, and clubhead velocity were measured.

**Comparator**: None.

**Duration**: Single testing session. Each golfer performed 10 driver shots after their own warm-up, with one minute rest between shots. Five trials per golfer were analysed based on data quality and high subjective shot-quality ratings.

**Outcome measures**:

- **Centre of pressure (COP)**: measured using two Kistler force plates, one under each foot, sampling at 1000 Hz. A global COP was computed from both plates.
- **Centre of gravity (COG)**: estimated from 3D marker trajectories collected with Vicon at 250 Hz and processed using a 16-segment Visual3D model. Whole-body COG was calculated as the weighted sum of body segments, with the golf club included.
- **COP and COG directions**: medial-lateral (ML) and anterior-posterior (AP), expressed as percentage distance between foot landmarks.
- **Swing timing events**: take-away, top of backswing, impact, and mid-follow-through. Swings were time-normalised to 501 points.
- **Clubhead velocity**: measured with TrackMan launch monitor.
- **Subjective shot quality**: 1–10 golfer rating, used to help select analysed trials.

**Key findings**:

- COP varied more than COG. COP required 4–5 principal components to explain 90% of variance, while COG required only 2 principal components.
- COP and COG PC1 scores were strongly correlated in both directions: ML approximately r = 0.90–0.92; AP r = 0.81. This suggests broad COP and COG movement patterns are related, especially in the medial-lateral direction.
- Clubhead velocity was predicted by three PCs, explaining 74% of variance: COPML PC3, COGML PC2, and COPML PC2.
- Higher clubhead velocity was associated with earlier COP movement toward the lead/front foot and greater/rate-of-change movement of COP toward the front foot during the downswing.
- Earlier COG movement toward the front foot was associated with lower clubhead velocity. This is important because COP shift and body-mass shift are not interchangeable.
- AP COP/COG components were not related to clubhead velocity.

**Statistical approach**:

- Principal component analysis (PCA) on time-normalised COP and COG curves.
- Pearson correlations between COP and COG PC scores.
- Stepwise mixed-effects linear regression, with individual golfer as a random effect, predicting clubhead velocity from standardised PC scores.

**Relevance to trial design**:

- **Appropriate training duration**: Does not directly inform training duration because there was no intervention.
- **Appropriate outcome measures**: Strongly supports using COP timing, COP magnitude/range, and COP rate of change rather than only a simple “weight transfer” value at impact.
- **Expected effect sizes**: No intervention effect sizes. The regression explained 74% of clubhead velocity variance, but this should not be treated as an expected training effect.
- **Likely confounders**: Skill level, individual swing style, trial selection based on subjective shot quality, clubhead velocity, and differences between COP movement and actual COG/body-mass movement.

**Limitations noted**:

- Cross-sectional design; cannot show that changing COP pattern through training improves performance.
- Small, skilled sample only; right-handed low-handicap golfers.
- Indoor laboratory setting hitting into a net, not on-course performance.
- Only five selected shots per golfer analysed.
- Performance outcome was clubhead velocity, not accuracy or scoring.
- Authors note that future studies should examine links between COP/COG PCs, kinematic variables, and other performance measures such as accuracy.

**Quality assessment**: Medium-high for biomechanical measurement; medium for direct trial-design relevance.

Reason: strong instrumentation, clear COP/COG processing, and time-series PCA rather than crude discrete events. However, this is not an intervention study, has a small homogeneous sample, and does not test whether training changes these measures.

**Direct quotes worth keeping**:

> “The relationship between COPML and COGML PC1 scores identified extremes of COP and COG patterns in golfers and could indicate a golfer’s dynamic balance.”

> “Golfers with earlier movement of COP to the front foot (PC2) and rate of change (PC3) patterns in ML COP, prior to the downswing, may be more likely to generate higher clubhead velocity.”

> “Golfers that moved their COG towards the front foot earlier in the backswing, however, were more likely to have lower clubhead velocity.”

**Flag anything that directly contradicts assumptions in our experiment design**:

This paper directly warns against treating “weight transfer” as a single simple construct. COP movement, COG movement, and coach-observed weight transfer are not the same. In this study, earlier COP shift toward the front foot related to higher clubhead velocity, while earlier COG shift toward the front foot related to lower clubhead velocity.

The trial should therefore avoid saying “more forward weight transfer is better” unless it defines whether it means COP, COG, vertical force, pressure distribution, or kinematics. The design should also avoid using a single impact-position metric as the only balance/weight-transfer outcome, because this paper suggests timing and rate of change are key.
