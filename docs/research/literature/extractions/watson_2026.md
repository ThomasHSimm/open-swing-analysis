# Watson et al. 2026 — GRF and CoP During the Golf Swing: Systematic Review

**Citation**: Watson, A.; Murray, A.; Ehlert, A.; Xu, J.; Williams, S.; Spiegelhalter, A.; Coughlan, D.; Turner, A.; Bishop, C. Ground Reaction Force and Centre of Pressure During the Golf Swing and Associations with Clubhead Speed and Skill Level: A Systematic Review. *Sports Medicine*, 2026.

**DOI**: 10.1007/s40279-025-02391-3

**Study type**: Systematic review (PRISMA 2020). No meta-analysis — narrative synthesis only. Not registered with PROSPERO.

**Population covered**: 24 included studies. Mixed populations across studies: amateur to professional, handicap ranges from <0 to 30+, ages 16–65. Most studies male-only or male-dominant. Sample sizes per study: 3–308 participants.

**Intervention**: Not applicable — review of observational, correlational, and cross-sectional studies. No intervention studies included. All included studies measured GRF and/or CoP during the golf swing using force plates.

**Comparator**: Varied by included study — skill level comparisons (skilled vs less skilled), club type comparisons, stance/ball position manipulations.

**Duration**: Search conducted September 2024. Publication date February 2026. Studies published from 1983 to 2024.

**Outcome measures across included studies**:
- Vertical GRF (peak, relative to body weight, at discrete swing phases)
- Mediolateral GRF (towards/away from target)
- Anteroposterior GRF (forward/backward)
- CoP range (mediolateral displacement, in metres)
- CoP velocity (mediolateral, m/s)
- CoP position at discrete swing events
- Motor moment (GRF of both feet at CoM)
- Angular impulse
- CHS (clubhead speed, m/s or mph)
- Ball speed, driving distance, handicap

**Measurement tools**: All 24 studies used force plates. 13/24 (54%) also used 3D motion capture. No standardised measurement protocol across studies. Most common swing position reference: discrete timepoints (takeaway, mid backswing, top of backswing, early downswing, mid downswing, ball contact, mid follow-through) — but definitions varied between studies, limiting direct comparison.

**Key findings**:

*CoP and CHS associations*: Nine studies showed moderate-to-strong relationships between CoP/GRF and CHS. Key correlations:
- CoP mediolateral range and CHS: r=0.53 (Ball & Best 2007), r=0.54 (Ball & Best 2007b)
- CoP velocity (mediolateral) and CHS: r=0.54
- Mediolateral CoP and CHS: R²=0.54 driver, 0.56 5-iron (Jones et al. 2023, n=104)
- Mediolateral CoP and handicap: R²=0.34–0.35 (Jones et al. 2023)
- Stance width and CHS: r=0.47
- Combined vertical + horizontal GRF and CHS: R²=0.70 (Bourgain et al.)
- Motor moment (GRF both feet at CoM) and CHS: r=0.83 (Bourgain et al.)
- **Peak lead foot GRF and ball speed: R²=0.85; peak lead foot GRF and driving distance: R²=0.74** (McHugh et al. 2024) — strongest single predictor found

*Skill level differences*: More skilled golfers consistently exhibited higher GRF and CHS. Key specific findings:
- Skilled golfers demonstrate greater unweighting of lead foot prior to impact (harder push into floor before ball contact)
- Skilled golfers: greater transfer of vertical GRF from trail to lead leg during downswing; peak vertical GRF occurs *earlier* in the downswing
- Lead foot vertical GRF during downswing: skilled 33±19% BW vs less skilled 59±28% BW (Okuda et al.) — note counterintuitive: less skilled show *higher* lead foot GRF during downswing, likely due to early weight shift or casting
- Trail foot vertical GRF during backswing: skilled 92±12% BW vs less skilled 76±13% BW
- 84% of novice golfers fail to demonstrate normal weight transfer pattern (lead leg shift before impact) — Koslow 1994

*CoP patterns*: No single technical model exists. Ball & Best identified "front foot" and "reverse" swing styles; Jones et al. showed a continuous distribution is more appropriate than discrete categories. CoP patterns consistent across clubs (driver, 3-iron, 7-iron).

*Slope effects*: CoP shifted 9.4% closer to front foot on downhill lies; 8.9% closer to trail foot on uphill lies. CoP pattern during downswing remained largely unchanged regardless of slope (Blenkinsop et al.).

*GRF variability*: Skilled golfers show lower GRF variability than less skilled (Jones et al. 2021, n=104). GRF reliability data largely absent from the literature — flagged as a critical gap.

**Statistical approach across included studies**: Correlational and cross-sectional designs. ANOVA, regression, principal component analysis, statistical parametric mapping (Kim et al. 2023). Quality assessed with Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) — all 24 studies scored 7 or 8 out of 9. No study reached maximum score; no study scored below 7.

## Relevance to Trial Design

**Outcome measure justification**: This review directly justifies CoP (mediolateral range and velocity) and lead foot peak vertical GRF as primary outcome measures for a golf intervention study. The strongest predictor of ball speed and driving distance is peak lead foot GRF (R²=0.85 and 0.74 respectively — McHugh et al. 2024). CoP mediolateral range explains ~54% of CHS variance. Both are established, validated metrics.

**Timing matters, not just magnitude**: The review explicitly notes that effective GRF use is dependent on *timing*, not solely magnitude. Skilled golfers produce peak vertical GRF *earlier* in the downswing. This directly supports measuring CoP timing as your primary outcome, not just peak values. This is a point the review makes forcefully and that no existing intervention study has operationalised.

**Expected effect sizes for baseline discrimination**: The difference between skilled and less skilled golfers provides a reference range for what CoP/GRF changes are meaningful. Lead foot vertical GRF during downswing differs by ~26 percentage points of body weight between groups (Okuda et al.). CoP mediolateral range accounts for R²=0.54 of CHS variance. These are the benchmarks against which intervention-induced changes should be assessed.

**GRF reliability is unknown**: The review explicitly flags this as a critical gap — "the reliability of GRF data is substantially less clear." This is directly relevant to your trial design: you need to establish or cite test-retest reliability for your force measurement protocol before interpreting pre-post changes. Without this, you cannot distinguish true change from measurement noise.

**Impulse is unexamined**: The review calls out the near-total absence of impulse measurement in golf GRF research, arguing it is mechanistically more important than peak force. This is a potential additional outcome measure worth considering.

**No intervention studies included**: All 24 studies are observational/cross-sectional. The review directly calls for intervention studies: "future investigations might consider examining whether changes in CoP strategies are trainable factors for enhancing golfer CHS." Your trial addresses this gap directly.

**Continuous analysis recommended**: The review recommends statistical parametric mapping over discrete timepoint analysis to capture "periods of relevance." This has implications for how you analyse CoP trajectories — discrete peaks may miss the timing information that differentiates skill levels.

## Limitations

**Noted by authors**: No single standardised measurement protocol across studies; inconsistent terminology for swing phases and GRF directions; no reliability data for GRF measures; no impulse data; small sample sizes in many studies (4 studies had <10 participants); predominantly cross-sectional designs preclude causal inference; no intervention studies.

**Additional limitations not acknowledged**: No PROSPERO registration; narrative synthesis without pooled effect sizes limits precision; quality assessment with NOS was chosen post hoc (noted by authors) and all studies scored 7–8, suggesting limited discriminative power of the tool; predominantly male populations limits generalisability to female golfers; most studies used laboratory settings with simulated ball striking into nets, which may not reflect on-course mechanics.

---

## Quality Assessment

**High** as a systematic review. PRISMA 2020 compliant, dual-reviewer screening, established quality assessment tool, comprehensive coverage of the golf GRF/CoP literature. The absence of meta-analysis is appropriate given heterogeneity. The honest acknowledgement of field-wide limitations (no reliability data, no impulse data, no intervention studies) is credible. Authors include Bishop (Middlesex/R&A), Murray (R&A/European Tour) — this is the authoritative group in UK golf science.

## Direct Quotes Worth Keeping

> "Effective use of GRF in the swing is also dependent on timing, not just solely on the magnitude of force produced."

> "The reliability of GRF data is substantially less clear, which would affect how the measures are used or interpreted across repeated swings or sessions. This represents an important step in understanding the usability of GRF data, so that practitioners have an appreciation of what represents a player's natural bandwidth of variability."

> "Future investigations might consider examining whether changes in CoP strategies are trainable factors for enhancing golfer CHS. Specifically, while alterations to CoP are of course possible (by virtue of changes in technique), it is unclear whether an increased change in CoP velocity would elicit positive adaptations to proxy measures such as CHS and distance off the tee."

> "84% of novice golfers failed to demonstrate the 'normal' weight transfer pattern recommended by golf coaches (a shift in weight onto the lead leg before impact), often resulting in a reverse pivot or incomplete weight shift."

> "No single technical model exists for how a golfer interacts with the ground and swings the club."

## Flags for Trial Design

1. **Your primary outcome (CoP timing) is directly called for by this review.** The explicit statement that timing matters and that continuous analysis would better capture periods of relevance is the strongest available justification for your trial's design choice. Cite this prominently in your rationale.

2. **You must address GRF reliability before or within your trial.** The review identifies absence of reliability data as the field's most pressing gap. If you don't establish test-retest reliability of your force measurement protocol, reviewers will flag it. Either cite an existing reliability study for your specific equipment/protocol, or build a reliability session into your design.

3. **McHugh et al. 2024 (R²=0.85 for peak lead foot GRF → ball speed) is your strongest effect size reference.** This gives you a concrete anchor for why lead foot GRF is the right primary outcome, and provides a basis for power calculation reasoning if you frame your expected effect relative to the skilled/less-skilled gap.

4. **The novice weight transfer failure rate (84%) is useful population framing.** If your participants are recreational/intermediate golfers, this stat supports the claim that your population has meaningful room for improvement — i.e. you're unlikely to hit ceiling effects.

5. **No intervention study exists in this 24-study literature.** This is your clearest novelty claim. The review was published February 2026 and explicitly identifies this as a gap. Your trial, if designed well, fills it directly.

6. **Queen et al. 2013 (handicap <9 vs >9, n=28)** is the most directly relevant population comparison for an intermediate golfer intervention — lower handicap showed greater *and earlier* force generation. The timing element specifically supports your outcome definition.
