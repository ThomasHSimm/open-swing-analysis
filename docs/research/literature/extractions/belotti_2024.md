# Belotti et al. 2024 — The Ground-X-Factor: Foot Proprioception and Golf Swing Performance

**Citation**: Belotti, A.; Bisazza, N.; Calcari, A.; Perrone, P.; Biscarini, A. The Ground-X-Factor: Training the Proprioceptive and Propulsive Function of the Foot for Improved Technique and Enhanced Performance of the Golf Swing. *Preprints.org* 2024.

**DOI**: 10.20944/preprints202411.2224.v1

**⚠️ Status: PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed. Posted November 2024. Treat findings as preliminary.**

**Study type**: Experimental, single-group repeated-measures (no control group). Pre-post within-subject design across three time points.

**Population**: n=25 healthy male golf players, 100% right-handed. Age 29 ± 10 years, height 181 ± 8 cm, weight 78 ± 9 kg. Handicap index 0.4 ± 2.7 (near-scratch to +3 — this is high-level competitive golf, well above the Li et al. population). Golf experience 20 ± 10 years, practice 16 ± 12 hours/week. Recruited from Italian golf clubs. At least 5 years competitive experience; 2+ hours/week practice minimum for inclusion.

**Intervention**: A 3-minute foot proprioceptive conditioning exercise. The exercise involves a rhythmic, four-phase sequence of forefoot pressure and lateral shearing forces alternating between lead and trail feet, performed from the standard golf address position. The sequence is designed to load and release torsional energy through the lower limb kinetic chain in a bottom-up pattern. Three progressive minutes: slow familiarisation → faster consolidation → swing-tempo execution. Participants were then instructed to integrate this foot motor sequence into their swing during subsequent test sessions.

**Comparator**: None. Single group with three time points (t0 = pre-intervention baseline; t1 = immediately post-intervention; t2 = 3 minutes after t1). No control group, no sham intervention.

**Duration**: Acute — the entire protocol (baseline swings, 3-min intervention, two post-intervention swing sessions) was completed in a single session. This is not a training study in the conventional sense; it tests immediate and very short-term retention of a motor pattern cue.

**Outcome measures**:
- **Vertical ground reaction force (vGR)**: peak vGR for lead foot, trail foot, and combined, at 7 defined swing positions (P1–P7) via Smart2Move Dual Force Plate system. Force expressed as % body weight. Bi-axial force plate (vertical component only — no horizontal/shear components captured).
- **Club head speed** (m/s) at ball impact via TrackMan launch monitor (dual Doppler radar + 4600fps cameras)
- **Ball speed** (m/s) after impact via TrackMan
- Note: Many other parameters were recorded by both systems but *not analysed* — this is explicitly acknowledged as a limitation.

**Key findings**:

*Lead foot vGR*: Significant increase t0→t1 (p<0.001; CI 3.94–10.27% BW). No further significant increase t1→t2. Total vGR showed a significant increase only over the full period t0→t2 (p=0.02).

*Trail foot vGR*: No significant effect of intervention at any time point.

*Club speed*: Significant increase t0→t1 (p<0.001; mean difference 0.65–1.53 m/s) and continued increase t1→t2 (p=0.02; mean difference 0.28–1.01 m/s). Total t0→t2 increase: ~2.7 m/s mean (CI 1.18–2.28 m/s in one-sample t-test).

*Ball speed*: No significant change t0→t1 (p=0.66). Significant increase t1→t2 (p=0.02; CI 0.43–2.42 m/s). Authors interpret this as: immediate technique change disrupts impact precision (centredness of strike), but within 3 minutes of practice the motor pattern integrates and ball speed recovers and improves.

**Temporal pattern interpretation**: Foot adaptation occurs immediately (t0→t1); club speed follows (t0→t1 and t1→t2); ball speed lags — only emerges at t2. Authors frame this as progressive propagation up the kinetic chain.

**Statistical approach**: One-way repeated-measures ANOVA (3-level: t0, t1, t2) with Scheffé post-hoc. Sphericity tested (Mauchly), normality (Shapiro-Wilk), homogeneity (Levene). Greenhouse-Geisser/Huynh-Feldt corrections applied where needed; ln transformation used where necessary. Supplemented with one-sample t-tests on individual pairwise differences (null: mean=0). p<0.05 throughout. Origin software.

## Relevance to Trial Design

**Training duration**: This is an *acute* intervention — 3 minutes of exercise, tested within a single session. This is categorically different from a multi-week training study. It demonstrates that foot proprioceptive cues can immediately alter vGR and club/ball speed, but says nothing about whether sustained training over weeks produces durable changes. For your trial design (multi-week intervention with pre/post measurement), this paper supports the plausibility of the mechanism but not the durability of the effect.

**Outcome measures — most relevant paper for your purposes**: This is the only paper in your list that measures vGR *during the golf swing* using force plates. Key details:
- Smart2Move Dual Force Plate: portable, monoaxial (vertical only), suitable for on-course use
- 7 defined swing positions provide time-resolved data, not just peak values
- Lead foot peak vGR occurs between P5 and P6 (downswing, shortly before club parallel to ground)
- Peak vGR at that position ~152–199% body weight in the appendix example
- CoP trajectory data also captured (lateral weight distribution, foot flare, stance width) but not analysed in this paper — these are directly relevant to your intended CoP timing outcome

**Expected effect sizes**: Acute effects on lead foot vGR: ~7% BW increase (CI 3.94–10.27% BW) immediately post-intervention. Club speed: ~1.1 m/s increase (t0→t1), ~1.7 m/s total (t0→t2). Ball speed: ~1.6 m/s total (t0→t2). These are *acute* effect sizes from a 3-min intervention in near-scratch golfers — they give a lower bound for what a multi-week training study might achieve, but direct comparison is not justified given the completely different populations and time scales.

**Likely confounders**:
- No control group — cannot rule out learning/warm-up effects across the three sessions
- t1 and t2 are separated by only 3 minutes; order effects, fatigue, and carry-over are all present
- High-level golfer population (handicap ~0.4) may show different (likely smaller) effects than recreational golfers due to already-optimised mechanics
- Participants were given explicit cognitive instructions to integrate the foot motor pattern, which may produce demand characteristics
- Only 5 swings per session — measurement precision uncertain
- No test-retest reliability data reported for any outcome measure in this population/protocol

## Limitations

**Noted by authors**:
- Near-scratch golfer population limits generalisability to less experienced players
- Only 3-minute intervention — novices may need longer
- Limited parameter selection despite rich dataset — many recorded variables not analysed
- Force plates were monoaxial (vertical only) — horizontal shear forces, which are mechanistically central to the intervention, not captured
- 6-iron only — generalisability to other clubs not confirmed

**Additional limitations not acknowledged**:
- No control group is the most serious gap — without it, you cannot rule out that swings 6–10 are simply better than swings 1–5 due to warm-up/practice, independent of the foot intervention
- The "3 minutes of reflection" between t1 and t2 is a confound: participants were asked to mentally rehearse the new technique, making it impossible to separate proprioceptive conditioning effects from cognitive cueing effects
- Only between-phase differences are tested; no reliability or minimal detectable change data for vGR or club/ball speed in this protocol
- The authors run both ANOVA post-hoc tests and one-sample t-tests on difference scores — these are not fully independent analyses; the discrepancy between them (e.g. total vGR t0→t1 is NS by ANOVA but significant by t-test) is not adequately explained and raises multiplicity concerns
- Preprint status: no peer review has occurred

## Quality Assessment

**Low–Medium** (preprint, no control group, acute protocol only, high-level population). Mechanistically interesting and the closest methodological precedent for measuring vGR during the swing in an intervention context. However, the absence of a control group and the acute-only design mean causal inference about the proprioceptive intervention is weak. The methodology for vGR and launch monitor measurement is solid and well-described.

## Flags for Trial Design

1. **Smart2Move is your most relevant measurement precedent.** The appendix screenshots show exactly what a vGR + CoP output looks like at each of 7 swing phases. This gives you concrete reference data for what lead foot vGR looks like across the swing (~20–30% BW at address rising to ~150–200% BW at peak downswing). Your CoP *timing* outcome is not explicitly extracted here, but the system captures it and the 7-position framework provides a natural way to define timing windows.

2. **No control group is a design gap you must not replicate.** The most serious weakness here. Even a waitlist control or an active comparator (e.g., matched volume of non-foot-specific balance exercises) would substantially strengthen your design relative to this paper.

3. **Acute vs. training study — this paper is not a training study.** It shows immediate motor learning from a 3-minute cue. Your trial asks a different question: does repeated proprioceptive training over weeks durably change CoP patterns and swing performance? These are related but distinct questions. The mechanism is supported; the duration is not.

4. **Population mismatch with Li et al. is significant.** Near-scratch golfers here vs. handicap 15–20 in Li et al. Near-scratch golfers already have well-optimised mechanics, making large proprioceptive gains less likely. If your population is recreational/intermediate (handicap 10–20), Li et al. is a better reference point for expected effect sizes. Be explicit about this in your design rationale.

5. **Only vertical GRF measured, but the intervention explicitly targets horizontal shear forces.** The authors acknowledge this. If your trial uses similar portable force plates, the same limitation applies. Triaxial platforms would be more informative but less practical on-course. Worth stating this explicitly in your methods and limitations.

6. **5 swings per session is a small measurement window.** With high shot-to-shot variability in recreational golfers, consider whether more swings per session (e.g., 8–10) would give more reliable mean values for your primary outcome.
