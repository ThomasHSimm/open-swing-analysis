# Yang et al. (2024) — Iron Shaft Weight and Golf Swing Performance

> **Triage note:** Not one of the 5 priority papers. Intervention is *shaft weight*, not balance training — so not a methodological precedent for the intervention itself. BUT directly relevant on construct: it measures **CoP/COG displacement during the swing on a force plate** and reports **handicap-group differences in weight transfer**. Main value: (a) a force-plate COG-outcome precedent, (b) a null result for a club manipulation on COG, (c) a worked example of the magnitude-vs-timing gap your design should exploit.

## Citation
Yang C-C, Chang C-C, Chao T, Tai H-L, Tsai Y-S. The effects of different iron shaft weights on golf swing performance. *Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology.* 2024;12:1343530.

## DOI
10.3389/fbioe.2024.1343530 (Open Access, CC BY)

## Study type
Original research; within-subject repeated-measures (3 shaft weights) crossed with a between-subjects skill grouping (LH vs HH). 2×3 mixed-design ANOVA. Lab-based biomechanics, not a training intervention.

## Population
20 male, right-handed amateur golfers, aged 20–30, injury-free (no muscle/joint injury in prior 6 months).
- **Low-handicap (LH):** n=10, handicap 4.3 ± 2.4, experience 8.7 ± 1.7 yr, age 21.2 ± 1.0
- **High-handicap (HH):** n=10, handicap 29.1 ± 5.4, experience 2.7 ± 2.8 yr, age 25.3 ± 3.4
- **Confound flag:** groups differ significantly in age (p=0.002) and experience (p<0.001), not just handicap. Height/weight not significantly different.

## Intervention
Manipulation = 7-iron **shaft weight**: light (77 g), medium (98 g), heavy (114 g). Clubhead (PING G425), shaft length, and grip standardised; shaft flex (S) constant; swing weight varied as a consequence (C8 / D0.5 / D1). 15 practice shots per club to acclimatise, 5-min rest, then 5 test shots; best 2 of 5 analysed.

## Comparator
Within-subject: the three shaft weights compared against each other. Between-subject: LH vs HH.

## Duration
**Single session.** No training period — immediate equipment effect only. *Provides no guidance on training duration.*

## Outcome measures
Three measurement systems run concurrently:
1. **Kinematics** — 9-camera Motion Analysis (Raptor-E, 200 Hz), Cortex 3.0. Max rotational angles (upper torso, pelvis, X-factor, right wrist hinge) at top of backswing; max angular velocities of same from downswing to impact.
2. **Weight transfer** — AMTI force plate (1000 Hz), synchronised. **Horizontal COG displacement**: address→farthest right, address→farthest left, medial-lateral range, anterior-posterior range. *Note: magnitude of COG shift, both feet on one plate — not CoP timing, and not per-foot loading.*
3. **Ball/club performance** — FlightScope Xi Doppler radar: clubhead speed, ball speed, carry, total distance, launch angle. Authors cite reliability ICCs (CHS >0.87; ball speed 0.89–0.98; carry 0.86–0.97; total 0.86–0.98; launch 0.86–0.93).

## Key findings
**Shaft weight (performance):** lighter shaft → higher CHS (light > heavy, p=0.022), higher ball speed (light & mid > heavy, p=0.004), greater carry (light & mid > heavy, p=0.008). Total distance showed a group×shaft interaction (p=0.029): in LH only, light > mid (p=0.019) and light > heavy (p=0.014, survives Bonferroni). HH benefit from lighter shaft less pronounced.

**Shaft weight (mechanics & COG):** **no significant effect** of shaft weight on any backswing angle, downswing angular velocity, or COG displacement (one pelvic-velocity interaction appeared but did not survive post-hoc Bonferroni). Authors' interpretation: golfers gained distance from lighter shafts *without altering major swing pattern or weight shift*.

**Skill-group differences (across all shaft weights):**
- LH > HH: max rightward upper torso rotation (p=0.008), max X-factor (p=0.007), max right wrist hinge (p=0.004), max upper torso angular velocity (p=0.017), max right wrist angular velocity (p=0.026), ball speed (p=0.001), carry (p=0.001).
- HH > LH: launch angle (p<0.001); **rightward COG displacement (p=0.002)** — HH shift more weight to the trail/right side. No group difference in leftward, M-L, or A-P COG displacement.

## Statistical approach
Independent t-tests for demographics; 2×3 mixed-design ANOVA (group × shaft) for all swing/COG/performance variables; α=0.05; Bonferroni post-hoc for simple main effects (corrected threshold p<0.017). SPSS v25.

## Relevance to trial design
- **Appropriate training duration:** None — single-session equipment study.
- **Appropriate outcome measures:** Useful precedent that **force-plate COG displacement during the swing is a viable, reportable outcome**, and that FlightScope ball/club metrics are reliable companions. BUT the COG metric here is *displacement magnitude*, not timing. Your study targets CoP timing AND magnitude — the timing dimension is exactly what this paper omits and what its own cited literature (Richards 1985; Queen 2013; Okuda 2010) says best discriminates skill. So: cite as outcome-measure precedent, but position your timing measures as filling a gap, not replicating this.
- **Expected effect sizes:** No formal effect sizes reported (ANOVA p-values only). The relevant prior is qualitative: a manipulation strong enough to move ball speed/distance produced **no detectable COG change** at n=10/group. Treat as a caution about sensitivity, not a usable effect-size anchor.
- **Likely confounders surfaced (directly applicable to your design):** handicap is confounded with age and experience here; weight transfer differs by skill; both-feet-on-one-plate setup cannot separate per-foot loading. If your study compares skill groups or uses a single force plate, these are your confounders too.

## Limitations noted by authors
Small sample, not generalisable; LH group younger but more experienced (experience confound acknowledged); shaft flex and swing weight not independently controlled (though they cite evidence flex doesn't materially affect CHS); participants not using own clubs (mitigated by tested weight range bracketing their normal clubs).

## Quality assessment
**Medium.** Methodologically clean instrumentation (validated kinematics + force plate + radar, sensible best-2-of-5, Bonferroni applied honestly). Pulled down for the project's purposes by: n=10/group, group contrast confounded by age/experience, single-session design, and a COG outcome that captures magnitude but not timing. Good equipment paper; thin as evidence about *trainable* weight-transfer change.

## Direct quotes worth keeping
Prefer paraphrase. One line worth verbatim for the timing-vs-magnitude point and skill prediction:
- On weight distribution predicting skill (their summary of Richards et al. 1985): heel-vs-toe weight at impact predicted skill level "with up to 85% accuracy."

## Contradiction flag
**Does not contradict your design, but supplies a sobering prior and a construct distinction you must keep clean.**
- **Sobering prior:** a club manipulation that shifted distance did *not* shift force-plate COG displacement at n≈10/group. If your balance intervention is modest and your outcome is COG *magnitude*, detection risk is real. Argues for (a) adequately powered n, (b) emphasising the **timing** outcome this paper neglected, where skill differences are reportedly larger.
- **Construct distinction:** "weight transfer / COG displacement" here = horizontal magnitude of whole-body COG, both feet on one plate. It is NOT CoP timing and NOT per-foot kinetics. When you cite this paper for "weight transfer," specify which sense, or you'll blur magnitude and timing the same way the static-balance issue blurred constructs in the Brennan extraction.
- **Useful corroboration:** HH golfers retain more weight on the trail side (greater rightward COG), consistent with Okuda/Queen. If your training aims to shift CoP toward the lead side / earlier, this is supporting baseline-difference evidence — just remember it's cross-sectional and confounded by experience.
